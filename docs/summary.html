Summary

The Richmond region’s housing picture in one page: who is arriving, what exists, what it costs, and who carries the cost. Each section links to the page with the figures and tables behind it. Trends compare the ACS 5-year windows ending 2014 and 2024 unless noted.

At a glance

The region is growing, and migration is doing the work

  • The region grew 10.7% to 1,341,248 people, led by Chesterfield (16.5%) and the four-locality expansion ring (15.5%); Richmond city grew 8.7% and Henrico 6.6%.
  • Natural increase fell from 4,228 people a year in 2010-2019 to 1,451 in 2020-2025, so net migration now supplies 90% of growth. In 10 of the 17 localities deaths exceeded births over 2020-2025.
  • International migration peaked at 10,787 in 2024; the latest year, 2025, was 5,387.
  • Richmond city flipped from domestic to international growth: its international migration rate rose from 2.8 to 6.8 per 1,000 a year while its domestic rate went from +5.3 to -1.1.

Full detail: Demographics

The population is older, more diverse, and more often living alone

  • The 65-and-over share rose from 12.9% to 16.7% while the under-18 share fell from 22.7% to 21.3%. The expansion ring is oldest at 20.0%.
  • The Hispanic or Latino share rose from 5.5% to 8.4%, and the White non-Hispanic share fell from 58.7% to 54.7%. Richmond city moved the other way: its White share rose from 39.7% to 41.4%.
  • 43.1% of Richmond city households are one person, against 61.4% married-couple families in the expansion ring. Region-wide, 29.6% of households are one person.
  • Real median household income in the Richmond MSA rose 9.6% to $86,679. Renters are far behind: 45.9% of renter households earn under $50,000, against 18.9% of owners.

Full detail: Demographics

The housing stock is single-family, older in the city, and built out in the suburbs

  • The three core localities held 404,918 housing units, up 13.3%; the expansion ring grew 18.4% from a much smaller base.
  • Single-family detached homes are 63% of the core’s stock and 88% of the ring’s. Only Richmond city is substantially multifamily (22% of units in buildings of 20 or more).
  • 38% of Richmond city’s housing predates 1950, against 2% in Chesterfield.
  • Vacancy is tight in the suburbs (3.8% in Chesterfield, 5.7% across the core) and highest in the southern localities (Sussex and Petersburg lead).
  • Permitting bottomed at 2,520 units in 2011, reached a series high of 10,414 in 2022, and was 9,091 in 2025. Multifamily outnumbered single-family permits in 3 of the last 4 years, and was 52% of 2025 units.

Full detail: Inventory

Prices rose faster than incomes; renters are no better off

  • After inflation, the Richmond MSA’s median home value rose 24.1% and median gross rent 11.3%, against 9.6% for median household income (the rent and income changes are within each other’s margins of error; the home-value change is not).
  • The typical home was worth 3.6 times the typical household income in the 2014 window and 4.1 times in 2024. Median rent equalled 20.0% of median income, from 19.7%.
  • Renter cost burden has not eased: 51.2% of renter households pay 30% or more of income on housing (51.4% in 2014), and 25.7% pay half or more. Owner burden fell from 26.1% to 20.0%.
  • Renter burden is now nearly identical across the core: 52.0% in Richmond city, 51.6% in Henrico, 52.2% in Chesterfield.
  • Ownership held near two-thirds (67.0%). Richmond city is the only core locality where renters outnumber owners (58,914 renter households to 45,407).

Full detail: Market

Evictions have not fully rebounded, and lending has settled below its 2021 peak

  • Eviction filings in the core three fell from 37,548 in 2016 to 7,575 in 2021 (80% lower), then recovered to 31,520 in 2025.
  • The latest 12 months run at 85% of the historical average, 15% below the 2016-19 monthly mean. Richmond city is furthest below baseline (81%) and Chesterfield closest (97%).
  • Home-purchase loans originated for principal residences peaked at 17,262 in 2021, fell to 10,607 in 2023, and were 10,877 in 2025. Counting refinances, the swings are larger (49,661 in 2021, 20,984 in 2025).
  • The home-purchase denial rate was 8.3% in 2025, against 8.2% in 2018. Over the last four years Black applicants were denied 16.1% of the time and Hispanic or Latino applicants 10.6%, against 4.5% for non-Hispanic White applicants.
  • Across all denied applications (all loan purposes, not only home purchase, 2022-2025), debt-to-income (35.8%) and credit history (32.9%) are the most-cited denial reasons.

Full detail: Market. Eviction and lending figures cover Richmond city, Henrico, and Chesterfield only. Filings are landlord court requests, not completed evictions, and the 2020-21 trough reflects pandemic moratoria and rental assistance. Lending counts cover one- to four-unit principal residences.

Lower-income renters carry the cost burden, and most live in Henrico and Chesterfield

  • 40.2% of households in the core three earn 80% of area median income (AMI) or less: 60.3% of renters and 28.7% of owners.
  • Richmond city has the highest share (50.9%, against 39.1% in Henrico and 33.4% in Chesterfield), but only 44.6% of the core three’s lower-income renter households live in the city.
  • 80.8% of renters at 30% of AMI or less pay half or more of income on housing, and 62.0% of renters at 51-80% of AMI pay 30% or more; above 120% of AMI, about 3% do (a small sample).
  • The lowest-income renters are mostly people living alone (58.5%) and are 55.9% Black; the lowest-income owners skew old (52.9% are 65 or over).

Full detail: Profiles. These figures come from household-level survey data (PUMS) for Richmond city, Henrico, and Chesterfield only, not the 17 localities used elsewhere on this page.

What to watch

Seven things to look out for
  1. Growth leans on migration. With natural increase down to 1,451 a year, a slowdown in international or domestic moves would show up directly in population growth. International migration in 2025 was 50% of its 2024 peak.
  2. Home values are outrunning incomes. Real home values rose 24.1% against 9.6% for income, and the value-to-income ratio widened from 3.6 to 4.1. The value measure is every owner’s own estimate, so it understates the cost of buying today.
  3. Half of renters are cost-burdened, and the gap by income is wide. The share did not improve over the decade, and the typical renter household earns far less than the typical owner household.
  4. Production is volatile and its mix keeps changing. Permits swing between single-family and multifamily from year to year, and the expansion ring continues to build almost entirely detached homes (88% of its stock) even though 30% of the region’s households are one person.
  5. Lower-income renters are where the burden concentrates. Renters at 30% of AMI or less number 30,599 households in the core three, and 80.8% of them pay half or more of income on housing.
  6. The outer suburbs are the oldest part of the region. The expansion ring has the highest 65-and-over share (20.0%) and, in aggregate, more deaths than births in 2020-2025 (-1,058), so its growth depends entirely on in-migration.
  7. Access to credit is uneven, and eviction filings are back near their old level. Black applicants were denied home-purchase loans 3.6 times as often as non-Hispanic White applicants, and eviction filings in the latest 12 months were at 85% of the historical average, after a trough of 7,575 filings in 2021.

For external affairs: connecting the data to Better Housing Coalition’s work

Better Housing Coalition (BHC) is the Richmond region’s largest nonprofit affordable housing developer and property manager. It provides affordable rental housing and homeownership, operates 8 senior affordable properties, and is working to do more mixed-income, mixed-use development. The figures below are regional public data; each bullet names the part of BHC’s work it speaks to.

Quotable numbers

Affordable rental housing

  • The core three have about 84,000 renter households at or below 80% of AMI, the income range where affordable rental housing is aimed. That is 60.3% of all renters, against 28.7% of owners.
  • Need is deepest at the bottom: 80.8% of renters at or below 30% of AMI pay half or more of their income on housing, and 62.0% of renters at 51-80% of AMI pay 30% or more. Above 120% of AMI, about 3% do (a small sample, so quote it loosely).
  • The burden is not easing region-wide: 51.2% of renter households pay 30% or more (51.4% in 2014), and real median rent rose 11.3% (±1.3 pts) while real median income rose 9.6% (±1.6 pts); those two changes are within each other’s margins of error, so do not say rent grew faster than income.
  • Housing stability is part of the case: filings in the core three are back to 85% of the historical average, about 2,600 a month, after falling to 7,575 for all of 2021. Filings are court requests, not completed evictions.
  • Where the need is: 55.4% of the core three’s lower-income renter households live in Henrico or Chesterfield, not Richmond city, even though the city has the highest share of lower-income households (50.9%).

Senior affordable housing

  • The region’s 65-and-over population grew from 12.9% to 16.7% of residents between the 2014 and 2024 windows, so the group BHC’s 8 senior properties serve is growing as a share of the region.
  • About 12,000 renter households in the core three have a householder 65 or over and income at or below 50% of AMI, 23% of all renter households in that range.
  • Older owners are exposed too: 52.9% of owner households at or below 30% of AMI have a householder 65 or over, roughly 17,500 older owner households at or below 50% of AMI.
  • The outer suburbs are the oldest part of the region (20.0% aged 65 and over in the expansion ring), with more deaths than births in 2020-2025, which points to a long-run need for senior housing outside the core.

Homeownership

  • The typical home was worth 3.6 times the typical household income in the 2014 window and 4.1 times in 2024; real median home value rose 24.1% against 9.6% for income.
  • About 70,000 owner households in the core three earn 80% of AMI or less (28.7% of owners), and 45.9% of renter households region-wide earn under $50,000, against 18.9% of owners. The gap between the two groups is the gap affordable homeownership has to bridge.
  • Access to credit is uneven: over 2022-2025, Black applicants were denied home-purchase loans 16.1% of the time and Hispanic or Latino applicants 10.6%, against 4.5% for non-Hispanic White applicants.
  • Across all denied applications (all loan purposes, 2022-2025; most denials in the lending data are refinance, cash-out, and home-improvement loans, so do not describe these as home-purchase denial reasons), debt-to-income (35.8%) and credit history (32.9%) lead, ahead of collateral and cash to close.
  • Lenders originated 10,877 home-purchase loans for principal residences in the core three in 2025, 37% below the 2021 peak of 17,262.

Mixed-income, mixed-use development

  • Richmond city is the one core locality where renters outnumber owners (58,914 renter households to 45,407) and the only one that is substantially multifamily, with 22% of units in buildings of 20 or more.
  • The suburbs are built almost entirely as detached homes: 63% of the core’s stock and 88% of the expansion ring’s, even though 30% of the region’s households are one person.
  • Production leans on multifamily in recent years: it outnumbered single-family permits in 3 of the last 4 years and was 52% of 2025 permitted units. Permits swing between 2,520 (2011) and 10,414 (2022) units a year.
  • Lower-income households are spread across the core, not concentrated in one place: 50.9% of households in Richmond city, 39.1% in Henrico, and 33.4% in Chesterfield are at or below 80% of AMI. That supports mixed-income communities in all three localities, not only in the city.
  • The region’s housing stock is old in the city (38% of Richmond city’s housing predates 1950) and tight in the suburbs (3.8% vacancy in Chesterfield), which limits the supply of affordable options without new construction.
Using these figures in communications
  • These are regional figures, not BHC results. They describe need and market conditions across Richmond city, Henrico, and Chesterfield (or the 17-locality region where noted). They do not count BHC’s residents, properties, or outcomes; pair them with BHC’s own program data.
  • Say “at or below 80% of AMI,” not “low-income,” without a definition. AMI bands here follow HUD income limits for the Richmond area; see Profiles.
  • PUMS counts are survey estimates. Household counts by income band carry sampling error, so quote them rounded (for example, “about 31,000 renter households at or below 30% of AMI”).
  • Senior counts are derived and rest on a small sample. They multiply the income-band count by the share of those households with a householder 65 or over (about 50 sample households for renters at or below 50% of AMI, margin of error ±1,100), so quote them as “about” and round to the nearest 500.
  • Lending rates are not sampled, but have caveats. HMDA counts reporting lenders’ applications, so the margin shown is only case-count noise. Denial rates exclude withdrawn and incomplete applications; about 16% of decided applications have no race or ethnicity recorded; lenders under the reporting threshold are not in the data.
  • The eviction baseline is the provider’s. The “percent of the historical average” has no documented definition. Prefer the count-based comparison (15% below the 2016-19 monthly mean) for anything external.
  • Check the wording of changes. Home values rose far faster than incomes (outside the margins of error); rent and income changes are statistically similar.
  • The data do not measure demand for mixed-use space. The mixed-income points rest on income, tenure, and structure-type patterns; retail and commercial demand needs separate evidence.
  • Eviction filings are not evictions. Lead with the trend against the pre-2020 baseline, not a count of displaced households.
Reading the numbers
  • Stock and production are different series. Stock (ACS) counts units standing; production (Building Permits Survey) counts permits issued, which lead completions and can be cancelled.
  • Small geographies are noisy. The four expansion-ring localities and Ashland carry wide margins of error; the pages suppress cells with a coefficient of variation above 30.
  • Income-band figures use a different geography. They come from PUMS, which reports only whole PUMAs; the seven used here tile Richmond city, Henrico, and Chesterfield exactly. The other localities are not covered, so these totals will not match the 17-locality pages.
  • Eviction and lending figures cover the core three only. Eviction filings are court records and lending figures are HMDA loan-level data; neither is ACS, so they will not reconcile with the 17-locality pages. Denial rates count denied / (originated + approved-not-accepted + denied) and are shown only where at least 100 applications were decided.
  • Migration figures are modeled from administrative records and revised each vintage. The 2010-2019 and 2020-2025 series come from different vintages and are compared, not joined.
  • Dollar changes are inflation-adjusted with national CPI-U, so they measure change relative to national prices, not a local cost-of-living index.
  • See Data Notes for sources and definitions.
